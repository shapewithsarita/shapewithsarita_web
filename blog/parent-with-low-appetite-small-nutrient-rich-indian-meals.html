Low appetite

My Parent Has a Low Appetite: Small, Nutrient-Rich Indian Meals

Sarita NawarCertified Nutrition & Fitness Coach
8 min readUpdated 18 Oct 2026

The short answer

  • A falling appetite is not just normal ageing. See a doctor to find the cause, especially if weight is dropping.
  • Meanwhile, offer 3 small meals and 2 or 3 snacks of nutrient-rich food: khichdi with ghee, milk, curd, eggs, paneer and nuts.
  • Never try to make a parent with low appetite lose weight.

"I am not hungry, beta. It is natural at my age." Many families hear this, and quietly worry. Eating less can be a sign of something treatable, and even small shortfalls can add up to muscle loss, weakness and falls. This guide shows how to help a parent with a low appetite using simple Indian meals, and when to take them to the doctor.

Why appetite drops

  • Dental problems, dry mouth or trouble chewing and swallowing.
  • Changes in taste and smell with age.
  • Medicines that cause nausea, dry mouth or a lack of hunger.
  • Constipation, acidity or digestive problems.
  • Low mood, loneliness or eating alone.
  • Illness, pain, anaemia or low vitamin B12.
  • Difficulty shopping or cooking, or memory problems.

See a doctor if

  • Your parent is losing weight without trying, about 5% or more over a few months to a year. For a 60 kg person, that is about 3 kg.
  • Appetite drops suddenly and stays low for a week or more.
  • There is coughing or choking while eating, or pain when swallowing.
  • There is repeated vomiting, diarrhoea, blood in the stool or severe constipation.
  • Your parent is unusually tired, confused, very low or has had a fall.

Clothes and watch straps that have become loose are early clues. Weigh your parent once a month, at the same time of day, and write it down.

The idea: small, frequent and nutrient-rich

  • Offer 3 small meals and 2 or 3 snacks, about every 2 to 3 hours while awake, instead of big plates that look overwhelming.
  • Make every mouthful count. Choose foods with calories and protein together, not low-calorie "diet" foods.
  • Do not fill up on tea, water or soup first. Offer fluids between meals.
  • Serve favourite foods and mild, familiar flavours.
  • Eat together whenever you can. Company increases appetite.

A day of small meals

  1. On wakingA small cup of warm milk or milk tea.
  2. BreakfastBesan chilla, upma or idli with sambar, and a katori of curd.
  3. Mid-morningMilk with mashed banana, or a handful of nuts and a fruit.
  4. LunchA small bowl of moong dal khichdi with ghee, curd and a soft sabzi.
  5. EveningA boiled egg or paneer cubes, or roasted chana, with buttermilk.
  6. DinnerSoft rotis with thick dal and sabzi, or khichdi again.
  7. BedtimeA small glass of warm milk, if tolerated.
Sarita's tip 🌿

Use a smaller plate and serve food in small katoris. A small portion that is finished feels like a win. A big plate that is left feels like failure.

If chewing or swallowing is hard

  • Choose soft foods that still carry protein: khichdi, dal, daliya, curd, egg bhurji, paneer bhurji, mashed vegetables and milk-based dishes.
  • Offer warm, moist food. Dry rotis and hard nuts can be difficult, so soak, grind or serve as powder.
  • Ask a dentist to check teeth and dentures.
  • If your parent coughs or chokes while eating, ask a doctor for a swallowing assessment.

Make mealtimes pleasant

  • Eat together at a table, with music or a chat.
  • Serve food while it is hot and fresh, with a nice aroma.
  • Choose the time of day when your parent is hungriest.
  • Ask what they would like to eat, and involve them in choices.
  • Keep hydration up between meals. See our guide to hydration for seniors.

What not to do

  • Do not force-feed or turn meals into arguments.
  • Do not put your parent on a "diet" or limit food to lose weight.
  • Do not wait weeks to see a doctor.
  • Do not start supplements or protein powders without a doctor's or dietitian's advice.

For sons and daughters

Be patient, avoid blame and eat with your parent when you can. Add protein to every meal. See how much protein seniors need, the Indian Senior Plate, and vegetarian protein and B12. Keep their doctor informed about any change in weight or appetite.

Frequently asked questions

Is a low appetite normal at 70 or 80?

Energy needs may fall a little with age, but unexplained weight loss or a sudden drop in appetite is not a normal part of ageing. If it lasts a week or more, or your parent is losing weight, see a doctor.

How many meals should my parent eat?

Many do well with three small meals and two or three snacks a day, rather than big meals. Offer something every 2 to 3 hours while they are awake, and focus on nutrient-rich foods.

What if my parent refuses to eat?

Do not force or argue. Offer small portions of favourite foods, eat together, change the time or place, and try softer or tastier options. If refusal continues, or there are swallowing problems, speak to a doctor.

Is it okay to add ghee when appetite is low?

A spoon of ghee can add useful energy to khichdi or dal for most people. If your parent has diabetes, heart disease or high cholesterol, ask the doctor or dietitian how much is right.

Can I give protein powder or nutrition drinks?

Food comes first. Add milk, curd, eggs, paneer, nuts and dal to meals. Nutrition supplements can help in some cases, but should be started on the advice of a doctor or dietitian.

How do I know if weight loss is serious?

Losing about 5% of body weight over a few months to a year without trying is a common warning sign. For a 60 kg person, that is about 3 kg. Loose clothes or a loose watch strap are early clues. Weigh monthly and see a doctor if weight is falling.
