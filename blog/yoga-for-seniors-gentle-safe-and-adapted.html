Yoga for Seniors: Gentle, Safe and Adapted

Sarita NawarCertified Nutrition & Fitness Coach
8 min readUpdated 20 Nov 2026

The short answer

  • Yoga can be safe and useful for seniors when it is gentle, seated or supported, slow and linked to comfortable breathing.
  • Avoid inversions, forceful breathing, breath retention, deep forward bends and deep twists.
  • Yoga supports balance and well-being. It is not a cure for any disease.

Yoga has deep roots in India, and many seniors have practised it all their lives. Others have been told it is "too much" at their age. Both are partly right. Yoga can be a lovely way to stay supple, calm and steady after 60, but the poses that suit a 30-year-old can hurt a 70-year-old. This guide shows what to do, what to avoid and how to adapt yoga with eight gentle movements from my handbook Stronger After 60.

What yoga can and cannot do

  • Reviews of studies suggest yoga gives small to moderate improvements in balance and mobility in older adults, and one large review found benefits for walking speed and frailty.
  • There is not yet good evidence that yoga on its own prevents falls. Exercise that includes balance and strength training has the strongest evidence, so combine yoga with those.
  • Chair-based yoga is a safe start, but standing practice with support helps balance more.
  • Yoga is not a cure for arthritis, diabetes, osteoporosis or any other disease. This book does not claim detoxification or reversing ageing.

Talk to your doctor first if

  • You have osteoporosis, a spinal fracture or severe back pain.
  • You have heart, blood pressure, eye (such as glaucoma), balance or neurological conditions.
  • You have felt dizzy or fainted, or have fallen recently.
  • You have had recent surgery or a joint replacement.

What to do: the five rules

  1. Use a chair or supportA sturdy chair without wheels, or a fixed counter, for any standing pose.
  2. Move slowly with your breathBreathe in and out comfortably. Never hold your breath.
  3. Keep the range smallStay comfortable. Stretching further is not better.
  4. Rest when you need toShort sessions, with breaks, beat long ones.
  5. Stay regularA few gentle minutes most days is better than one hard class.

Yoga has a rich Indian philosophical and physical tradition, and its cultural importance is respected here. This is about adapting the practice for older bodies, not replacing it.

A 10 to 15 minute yoga wake-up

Do the 8 movements in order as a morning "wake-up" routine. Move slowly and breathe comfortably throughout. For a shorter option, see our 10-minute morning routine.

  1. 2 minutesSeated tall posture and comfortable breathing.
  2. 3 minutesShoulder rolls, seated cat-cow (small range) and side reach.
  3. 3 minutesSeated marching and ankle circles.
  4. 2 minutesGentle trunk rotation, both sides.
  5. 2 minutesSlow breathing to finish.
Sarita's tip 🌿

Think of yoga as moving with your breath, not as achieving a pose. If it feels peaceful and you are not straining, you are doing it right.

If you have osteoporosis

Movement is still helpful, but spinal flexion (forward bending and rounding) and end-range twisting can increase the risk of vertebral fractures. Keep your spine tall, hinge from the hips if you must bend, and avoid inversions. Ask your doctor or physiotherapist for a list of safe movements. See our guide on bones after 60.

Choosing a class or teacher

  • Look for a teacher with experience teaching older adults and a chair option.
  • Choose slow-paced classes, such as gentle Hatha or restorative.
  • Tell the teacher about your health conditions before you start.
  • Avoid hot yoga, competitive classes and forceful hands-on adjustments.
  • Do not compare yourself with younger people in the room.

Add strength and balance

For the best results, combine yoga with strength and balance work. See our guides on strength training after 60, balance exercises and the 12-week plan.

For sons and daughters

Join your parent for a few sessions, check the chair, and encourage slow, comfortable movement. Praise consistency, not flexibility. See how to help your parents exercise safely.

Stop right away if

  • Chest pain, fainting or severe dizziness.
  • Unusual shortness of breath or sudden new weakness.
  • Sharp pain that gets worse.

Stop and seek medical help straight away if any of these happen.

Frequently asked questions

Is yoga safe for seniors?

It can be, when it is gentle, adapted and done at your own pace. Use a chair or support, move slowly with your breath, keep movements small, and skip anything that hurts. Check with your doctor first if you have a health condition.

Can seniors do pranayama?

Gentle, comfortable breathing is generally fine. Forceful breathing and breath retention are not recommended as general practices for older adults, because they can cause dizziness and strain. Ask your doctor before trying any advanced pranayama.

Can yoga cure arthritis, diabetes or osteoporosis?

No. Yoga can support balance, mobility and well-being, but it is not a cure for any disease. Keep following your doctor's treatment, and tell your doctor about your yoga practice.

Is chair yoga enough?

Chair yoga is an excellent and safe starting point. Over time, many people add supported standing balance work and strength exercises, because standing practice helps balance more than seated movements alone.

Can I do yoga if I have osteoporosis?

Often yes, with modifications. Avoid deep forward bending, rounding the spine and deep twists, and skip inversions. Ask your doctor or physiotherapist which movements are safe for you.

Which yoga poses should seniors avoid?

Headstand, shoulder stand and other inversions, deep forward bends and twists, forceful breathing and breath retention, sitting cross-legged on the floor if it strains your knees or back, and any pose held through pain.
