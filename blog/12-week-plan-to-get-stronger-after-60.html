A 12-Week Plan to Get Stronger After 60

Sarita NawarCertified Nutrition & Fitness Coach
9 min readUpdated 10 Nov 2026

The short answer

  • Follow a simple weekly schedule of strength, walking, balance and rest for 12 weeks.
  • Start easy: 15 to 20 minute sessions and one set of 5 to 8 repetitions. Change one thing at a time.
  • Do the readiness check and ask your doctor first if you have any health condition.

Most people who want to get stronger after 60 do not fail because the exercises are too hard. They fail because they do not know what to do on Tuesday, how much to do in week five, or when to make it harder. A plan fixes that. This is the 12-week plan from Chapter 9 of my handbook Stronger After 60: a weekly schedule, a step-by-step progression and a 10-minute starter day for the days when you have little energy.

Before you start

This plan is for a person who can exercise safely. Please do the 2-minute readiness check in our guide on exercises for elderly parents at home, and speak to your doctor first if any of these apply:

  • A recent fall, dizziness or fainting.
  • Chest discomfort, unusual breathlessness or new weakness.
  • Recent surgery, a fracture or a serious illness.
  • Osteoporosis, or heart, lung, kidney or neurological conditions.

What you need

  • A sturdy chair without wheels, and a fixed counter or railing.
  • Closed, non-slip footwear and a clear floor.
  • Optional: a light resistance band and two 500 ml water bottles.
  • A notebook or calendar to tick off your sessions.

The 12-week progression

The book divides the 12 weeks into four phases:

WeeksFocusHow
Weeks 1 and 2Learn techniqueEasy resistance and short sessions of 15 to 20 minutes. One set of 5 to 8 repetitions.
Weeks 3 and 4Build the habitAdd a few repetitions or a little time if recovery is good.
Weeks 5 to 8Gradual progressionIncrease duration, resistance or sets, one variable at a time.
Weeks 9 to 12ConsolidateKeep the routine, reassess your functional goals and choose your next target.

Here is a week-by-week way I suggest using that template. It is an example, not a prescription. Adjust it to how you feel.

WeekA suggested way to use the plan
1 to 22 strength days, 1 set of 5 to 8 reps. Short walks of 5 to 10 minutes. Sessions of 15 to 20 minutes.
3 to 4Add a few repetitions toward 8. Add a third strength day only if you recover well.
5 to 6Add 2 to 3 minutes to your walks or seated cardio. Keep strength the same.
7 to 8Move toward 8 to 12 repetitions, or add a second set. Change only one thing.
9 to 10Keep the routine. Repeat the 30-second chair stand test and compare with your first score.
11 to 12Reassess goals: stairs, shopping, grandchildren. Choose your next target.

How to progress your strength work

LevelSuggested volume
BeginnerOne set of 5 to 8 controlled repetitions may be enough.
IntermediateWork toward 1 or 2 sets of 8 to 12 repetitions.
Active senior2 or 3 sets may be appropriate when technique, recovery and health allow.
  • Change one thing at a time: repetitions, sets, resistance or range.
  • Add repetitions before resistance.
  • Rest 1 to 2 minutes between exercises, and do not train the same muscles on consecutive days. Studies suggest at least 48 hours between sessions.
  • Move slowly and never hold your breath. See our guide to strength training after 60.

The 10-minute starter day

If a full session feels too much, do this short routine and count it as a win.

  1. 2 minutesSeated marching (4) and shoulder rolls (2).
  2. 3 minutesSit-to-stand (9), one set.
  3. 2 minutesSupported heel raises (12).
  4. 2 minutesComfortable breathing (6).

If you cannot stand safely

Choose the seated exercises first: seated marching, shoulder rolls, ankle circles, seated band rows and presses, knee extensions and seated weight shifts. For people who cannot safely stand or transfer, bed-based exercises such as ankle pumps and heel slides exist, but need a plan from a healthcare team. See our chair exercises guide.

How to know it is working

  • Repeat the 30-second chair stand test every month and compare with your own earlier scores.
  • Notice daily life: standing up, climbing stairs, carrying shopping, walking longer.
  • Keep a simple log of walking minutes, strength days and energy.

The WHO suggests older adults work toward 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, with muscle-strengthening on at least two days, when health allows. Those are population-level goals, not where you must begin.

When to pause or slow down

  • Illness or fever.
  • Worsening pain.
  • Unusual tiredness.
  • A drop in balance or a fall.

Stop and seek medical help straight away for chest pain, fainting, severe dizziness, unusual shortness of breath or sudden new weakness.

Sarita's tip 🌿

Consistency is worth more than intensity. Rest is part of the plan, not a sign of failure. A short session you actually do beats a perfect session you skip.

For sons and daughters

Help your parent choose three fixed days, do the first sessions together and tick the calendar. Praise consistency, not the number of repetitions. Pair the plan with good food: see the Indian Senior Plate and how much protein seniors need.

Frequently asked questions

Is 12 weeks enough to get stronger after 60?

Many studies of older adults report strength improvements within about 6 to 8 weeks of regular training, and 12 weeks is a good time to reassess. Strength is kept by continuing, so treat the plan as the start of a habit, not a finish line.

Can I start if I have never exercised?

Yes, but begin gently. Do the readiness check, use the seated and supported versions, and start with 15 to 20 minute sessions and one set of 5 to 8 repetitions. Ask your doctor first if you have any health condition.

How long should each session be?

Start with 15 to 20 minutes and build up slowly. Short, regular sessions are better than long ones that leave you tired or sore for days.

What if I miss a week or fall ill?

Do not try to catch up. Pause progression during illness, restart at an easier level and build up again. Missing a week is normal and does not undo your progress.

Do I need a gym or equipment?

No. You need a sturdy chair without wheels and a fixed counter or railing. A light resistance band and two 500 ml water bottles are optional.

Can I follow this plan with a health condition?

Often yes, with adjustments, but it depends on the condition. The plan is a template, not a clinically validated programme, so please check with your doctor or physiotherapist first.
