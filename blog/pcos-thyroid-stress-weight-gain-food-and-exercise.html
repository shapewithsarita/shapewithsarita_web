Hormones and weight

PCOS, Thyroid and Stress Weight Gain: What Food and Exercise Can and Can't Do

Sarita NawarCertified Nutrition & Fitness Coach
10 min readUpdated 22 Nov 2026

The short answer

  • For PCOS, lifestyle is the first-line treatment, but no single "PCOS diet" is proven best. A balanced Indian plate, 150 to 300 minutes of activity and strength training all help.
  • For thyroid, treatment comes first. The tablet is not a weight-loss drug, and extra doses are dangerous.
  • Stress and poor sleep add to the problem. Food and exercise cannot cure PCOS or thyroid disease. See your doctor.
Please note: this is education, not diagnosis. Weight gain can have medical causes, so if something feels off, see your doctor. Never stop or change medicines on your own.

"I eat very little, I exercise, and the weight still will not move." I hear this often from homemakers and working women alike, and it is not in your head. Hormones matter. PCOS, an underactive thyroid and chronic stress can all make weight harder to manage. This guide explains, honestly, what food and exercise can do for each, what they cannot do, and when it is time to see a doctor.

How common is this?

  • PCOS affects roughly 8 to 13% of women of reproductive age worldwide, and up to 70% of cases are undiagnosed. Estimates in India vary widely between studies.
  • PCOS can affect women of any weight, not only women who are overweight.
  • Thyroid disorders, especially an underactive thyroid, are much more common in women than in men.
  • Stress and short sleep are very common among working women and homemakers alike.

PCOS: what the evidence says

  • Lifestyle is first-line. The 2023 international PCOS guideline recommends weight and lifestyle management, with diet, exercise and behaviour support, as the first step.
  • No single diet wins. Studies find similar results from different approaches, so choose a balanced plan you can keep.
  • Modest weight loss helps. Losing 5 to 10% of body weight is linked with better insulin resistance and metabolic health, and even 2 to 5% may improve ovulation and cycles in some women.
  • Exercise targets: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening. For weight loss, more, around 250 minutes a week, may be needed.
  • Be kind to yourself. Weight stigma and disordered eating are more common in women with PCOS, so avoid extreme diets.

The PCOS-friendly Indian plate

You do not need special foods. A balanced plate with fibre, protein and fewer sugary and fried items is the base. There is no need to cut out rice, roti or dairy completely. Detox teas, karela juice and "miracle" powders are not proven treatments.

My plate

  • Half the plate: non-starchy vegetables and salad (palak, lauki, bhindi, cabbage, cucumber, tomato).
  • A quarter: protein at every meal (dal, sprouts, paneer, egg, fish, chicken, soya, curd).
  • A quarter: whole grains in a measured portion (multigrain or jowar roti, ragi, brown or parboiled rice, oats).
  • A little healthy fat: nuts, seeds and measured oil or ghee.
  • Whole fruit in portions, not juice.
  • Sugary drinks, sweets, maida and deep-fried or packaged snacks kept for occasions.
  • Regular meal times, with no long gaps and no skipped breakfast.

My movement

  • Strength training 2 to 3 days a week: squats, push-ups, rows, glute bridges. Muscle helps insulin sensitivity.
  • Walking or other moderate activity most days, building toward 150 to 300 minutes a week.
  • Short bouts count: 10 minutes after each meal is fine.
  • Pick activity you enjoy: dance, cycling, swimming, brisk walks.
  • Leave at least one full rest day, and do not overtrain when stressed or short on sleep.
  • Aim for 7,000 or more steps a day, built up slowly.

My sleep and stress

  • Sleep 7 to 9 hours, with a fixed bedtime.
  • A 5-minute stress reset twice a day.
  • Dinner 2 to 3 hours before sleep.
  • Screens off 30 to 60 minutes before bed.

Questions for my doctor

  • Which tests should I have (thyroid, blood sugar or HbA1c, lipids, vitamin D, B12, hormones, ultrasound)?
  • Do I have PCOS, a thyroid problem or something else?
  • Is my medicine dose and timing right?
  • Do I need any supplement, and in what dose?
  • How much weight, if any, should I aim to lose, and how fast?
  • When should I come back for a review?
See your doctor. This checklist is general information, not a diagnosis. If you have irregular periods, excess hair, acne, trouble conceiving, unexplained weight gain, tiredness, hair loss or feel cold, please get checked. Do not stop or change any medicine on your own.

Thyroid: what to know

  • Get tested. A simple blood test (TSH) shows how your thyroid is working. Your doctor decides what else to check.
  • Weight gain is usually modest. A slow thyroid can add a few kilograms, partly fluid. Once treated, some of it may come off, but the tablet is not a weight-loss drug.
  • Never take extra thyroid medicine to lose weight. Too much can cause a racing heart, anxiety, poor sleep and bone loss.
  • Take levothyroxine as prescribed: on an empty stomach, usually 30 to 60 minutes before breakfast, tea or coffee.
  • Keep calcium, iron and antacids at least 4 hours away from the tablet, as they reduce absorption.
  • Tell your doctor if your weight changes a lot, because your dose may need review.

Stress and sleep: the quiet drivers

Stress raises cortisol and makes high-calorie food more tempting, while short sleep raises hunger hormones. You cannot remove stress, but you can protect 7 to 9 hours of sleep, take two 5-minute resets a day and keep your meals regular. What food and exercise cannot do

  • Cure PCOS or thyroid disease, or replace medicines.
  • Burn fat from one area, such as the belly, on its own.
  • Undo poor sleep or constant stress.
  • Produce fast, safe weight loss. A safe pace is slow.

See your doctor if

  • Your periods are irregular, very heavy or have stopped.
  • You have new excess hair, acne, hair thinning or dark patches of skin.
  • You are trying to conceive without success.
  • You are gaining weight quickly, or feel unusually tired, cold or constipated, or have hair loss.
  • You feel persistently anxious or low, or cannot sleep.

Your doctor can decide which tests and treatment are right for you, including medicines for PCOS or thyroid.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best diet for PCOS?

There is no single proven best diet for PCOS. Research suggests that balanced approaches with similar effects work, so the best diet is one you can keep. A plate with plenty of vegetables, enough protein, whole grains and measured healthy fats, plus regular meals and activity, is a sound base. Your doctor or dietitian can tailor it.

Can I lose weight with PCOS?

Yes, though it can be slower. Even a modest weight loss, around 5 to 10% of body weight, is linked with better insulin resistance, cycles and metabolic health. Regular strength training, walking, good sleep and steady meals all help.

Do I have to stop eating rice, roti or dairy?

Not necessarily. There is no strong evidence that you must cut out rice, wheat or dairy. Focus on portions, whole grains, fibre, protein and fewer sugary and fried foods. Do not remove food groups without advice.

Will thyroid medicine make me lose weight?

Treatment can correct weight gained from a low thyroid, often a few kilograms and partly fluid, but it is not a weight-loss drug. Never take extra thyroid medicine to lose weight. It can harm your heart and bones. Take it as prescribed and tell your doctor about big weight changes.

Can stress and poor sleep cause weight gain?

They can contribute. Stress and short sleep are linked to higher hunger, more cravings and less energy for activity. Protecting 7 to 9 hours of sleep and building small stress resets help your weight and your hormones.

How long will it take to see results?

Energy, sleep and cravings often improve within a few weeks. Weight and cycle changes usually take 3 to 6 months or longer. Judge progress by waist, energy, cycles and strength, not just the scale.
