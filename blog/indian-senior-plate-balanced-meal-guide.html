Senior nutrition

The Indian Senior Plate: Build a Balanced Meal Without Counting

Sarita NawarCertified Nutrition & Fitness Coach
7 min readUpdated 15 Nov 2026

The short answer

  • Fill half your plate with vegetables and fruit, a quarter with whole grains and a quarter with protein.
  • Add 1 to 2 katori of curd, buttermilk or milk daily, if you tolerate it.
  • Measure with your hand and a katori. No counting needed.

Counting calories is hard, tiring and not very Indian. Many seniors and their families give up on it. A simpler way is to look at the plate. The Indian Senior Plate shows how to build a balanced lunch or dinner with the roti, dal and sabzi you already eat, and a hand-and-katori guide shows how much. This guide also includes a free printable page for your fridge.

The idea in one picture

India's national "My Plate for the Day" from ICMR-NIN also puts vegetables and fruit at about half the plate, followed by grains and protein foods, with milk or curd alongside. The Indian Senior Plate follows the same idea for lunch and dinner:

  • About half: vegetables and fruit, including green leafy vegetables.
  • About a quarter: whole grains such as roti, millets and rice.
  • About a quarter: protein from dal, egg, fish, paneer or soya.
  • On the side: curd, buttermilk or milk.

Hands-and-katori portion guide

Food groupSimple portion guide
Cooked grains (rice, roti)1 roti is about 1 palm-sized disc. 1 katori of cooked rice is about 150 ml. Adjust to appetite and activity.
Dal and pulses1 katori at lunch and 1 at dinner.
Vegetables2 katori of cooked or raw vegetables across the day. Include green leafy vegetables often.
Fruit1 to 2 servings (about a fist-sized fruit each) daily.
Milk and curd1 to 2 servings daily, if tolerated.
Egg, fish, chicken, paneer, tofuA palm-sized portion (about 100 g cooked) once or twice a day, as preferred.
Nuts and seedsA small handful (10 to 15 g) daily.
Oil and gheeAbout 3 to 5 teaspoons in the whole day's cooking.

Smart cooking and label tips

  • Steam, boil, pressure-cook, roast or lightly saute instead of deep frying.
  • Use herbs and spices (haldi, jeera, ajwain, hing, lemon) for flavour instead of extra salt.
  • On packets, check salt, sugar and fat per serving, and prefer short ingredient lists.
  • Sip fluids through the day, about 1.5 to 2 litres, unless your doctor has restricted them.

How to build your plate in 3 steps

  1. Vegetables firstFill half the plate with sabzi, salad and greens.
  2. Add proteinA katori of dal or a palm-sized portion of egg, fish, paneer, tofu or soya.
  3. Add grains1 or 2 rotis, or a katori of rice or millets, and curd on the side.

Sarita's tip 🌿

Serve the meal on a plate with small katoris. It makes portions easy to see, and it looks like a normal Indian meal, not a diet.

Adjust the plate for your health

  • Low appetite: eat smaller, more frequent meals with nutrient-rich foods like milk, curd, eggs and khichdi. See a doctor for unplanned weight loss.
  • Diabetes: keep carbohydrate portions sensible, pair them with dal and vegetables, and coordinate meals with your medicines.
  • High blood pressure: go easy on salt, pickles, papad and namkeen. The WHO suggests less than 5 g of salt, about 1 teaspoon, a day.
  • Kidney disease: protein, potassium and fluids may need changes. Follow a renal dietitian's plan.
  • Chewing difficulty: choose soft foods that still carry protein, and ask a dentist or doctor to check.

For sons and daughters

Plan the week's sabzis, keep soft options ready and print the plate guide for the kitchen. Pair the plate with enough protein (see how much protein seniors need) and with strength work (see strength training after 60).

Ask your doctor or dietitian if

  • You have diabetes, kidney, heart or liver disease.
  • You have lost weight without trying or have a poor appetite.
  • You have trouble chewing or swallowing.
  • You take many medicines or have been told to limit fluids.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Indian senior plate?

It is a simple way to build lunch and dinner: about half the plate vegetables and fruit, about a quarter whole grains such as roti, millets or rice, and about a quarter protein such as dal, egg, fish, paneer or soya, with curd or milk daily.

How much roti or rice can a senior eat?

It depends on appetite, activity and health. As a guide, one roti is about one palm-sized disc and one katori of cooked rice is about 150 ml. Many people start with 1 or 2 rotis or a small katori of rice at a meal and adjust.

Is rice allowed for seniors with diabetes?

Often yes, in a sensible portion, paired with dal and vegetables. Plans differ by person and medicine, so follow your doctor's or dietitian's advice.

How many vegetables should seniors eat?

As a rough guide, about 2 katori of cooked or raw vegetables across the day, including green leafy vegetables often, plus 1 or 2 servings of fruit. Increase gradually if you are not used to the fibre.

How much oil or ghee is suitable in a day?

A rough guide is about 3 to 5 teaspoons of visible oil, ghee or butter in the whole day's cooking, adjusted to individual advice. People with heart disease or high cholesterol should follow their doctor's guidance.

Do seniors need to count calories?

Not usually. The plate and katori method is a practical start. A doctor or dietitian can help with exact targets if you have a health condition or unplanned weight loss.
