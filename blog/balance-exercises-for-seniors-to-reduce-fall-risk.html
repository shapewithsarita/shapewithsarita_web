Fall prevention

Balance Exercises for Seniors to Reduce Fall Risk

Sarita NawarCertified Nutrition & Fitness Coach
8 min readUpdated 23 Oct 2026

The short answer

  • Practise supported balance exercises with a fixed counter and someone nearby.
  • Regular strength and balance practice is one of the best-researched ways to reduce falls.
  • Fall-proof the home too, and ask your doctor first if you have fallen recently.

About one in three adults aged 65 and over falls at least once a year. Even a small fall can lead to a fracture, a hospital stay or a loss of confidence. The good news is that there is a lot you can do. Balance exercises for seniors, a safer home and the right footwear all help reduce fall risk. This guide shows safe, supported moves you can try at home.

Check with your doctor first if

  • You have fallen, or nearly fallen, in the past 6 months.
  • You feel dizzy or faint when you stand or exercise.
  • You use a walker, cane or wheelchair.
  • You have osteoporosis or a heart, nerve or other health condition.

People at higher risk of falling are best guided by a doctor or physiotherapist. A supervised programme is often safest.

Set up for safe balance practice

  • Hold a fixed kitchen counter or railing, not a light chair or anything with wheels.
  • Have someone nearby when you start.
  • Clear the floor of rugs, cords and pets.
  • Wear closed, non-slip footwear.
  • Never close your eyes or let go of support to make a move harder.
  • Stop and rest whenever you feel tired or unsteady.

Six supported balance exercises

Start with the first one if you cannot stand safely, and work down the list only as it feels comfortable.

ExerciseHow to do itStarting doseSafety tip
Seated weight shiftsSit on a stable chair. Shift your upper body a small distance to one side, return to centre, then the other side.5 to 8 per sideThe starting option for people who cannot stand safely.
Supported standing weight shiftsHold a fixed counter with both hands. Shift your weight gently from one foot to the other, keeping both feet planted.8 to 10 each wayStay seated if standing is unsafe.
Modified heel-to-toe stanceHold a counter. Place one foot slightly ahead of the other, with a comfortable gap. Hold steady, then switch.10 to 20 seconds per position, 2 to 3 timesDo not close your eyes or let go to make it harder without professional guidance.
Supported marchingHold a stable counter, lift one foot, lower it and alternate without rushing.10 to 20 steps, 1 to 3 roundsUse smaller lifts or march seated. Avoid loose rugs and quick turns.
Supported side steppingHold a counter, take a short step sideways and bring the other foot to a comfortable stance. Repeat the other way.3 to 5 steps each way, twiceKeep the floor clear. Do not cross your feet if that makes balance hard.
Supported single-foot liftHold a fixed counter with both hands. Lift one foot just off the floor, hold briefly, then switch.3 to 5 lifts per legOnly if you already stand securely. Not for unsupervised high-fall-risk people.

Home fall-proofing checklist for Indian homes

Most falls happen at home. Walk through each area with your parent or with someone who can help you.

AreaWhat to check
BathroomNon-slip mat, grab bar beside the toilet and shower, a stool for showering, night light, floor kept dry.
BedroomClear path to the toilet at night, a lamp or torch within reach of the bed.
Living areaNo loose rugs or trailing cords, furniture arranged for easy walking.
StairsHandrail on at least one side, good lighting, no clutter.
Kitchen and puja roomSpills wiped at once, daily items within easy reach, no climbing on stools or chairs.
FootwearClosed, non-slip footwear indoors. Avoid walking in socks or bare feet on smooth floors.
MonsoonWipe wet entrances, use a non-slip mat at the door and keep umbrellas and shoes out of walkways.

Walking safely

  • Wear supportive footwear and use any prescribed walking aid.
  • Choose a familiar, level and well-lit route.
  • Do not rush, especially when turning or stepping off a kerb.
  • Think about heat, traffic, uneven surfaces and drinking water, especially in summer and the monsoon.
Sarita's tip 🌿

Do your balance practice right after you make your morning tea, while you stand at the kitchen counter. The counter is already there for support, and the habit is easy to remember.

How often and how to progress

Experts suggest that strength and balance exercise works best when it is done regularly, often three or more times a week, and ideally kept up for life. Start with fewer sessions and add more slowly. Increase repetitions or time before you make a move harder, and ask a physiotherapist before reducing support.

Other things that affect falls

  • Ask your doctor to review your medicines, some of which can cause dizziness.
  • Get your eyes checked regularly and keep spectacles clean and up to date.
  • Stand up slowly from lying or sitting.
  • Do leg-strengthening exercises too, such as sit-to-stand and heel raises.

For sons and daughters

Help with the checklist, fit grab bars and night lights, and stay beside your parent for the first few balance sessions. Our guide on exercises for elderly parents at home shows how to do it safely. For seated exercises, see our chair exercises guide.

When to stop and get help

  • Chest pain, fainting or severe dizziness.
  • Unusual shortness of breath or sudden new weakness.
  • Sharp pain that gets worse, or a fall with injury.

Stop and seek medical help straight away if any of these happen.

Frequently asked questions

Can balance exercises really reduce falls?

Research reviews show that structured exercise, especially programmes that combine balance and strength training, can reduce falls in older adults. Results depend on the person, the programme and how regularly it is done.

Is it safe to practise balance at home?

It can be, if you hold a fixed support, have someone nearby, keep the floor clear and start with the easiest moves. If you have fallen recently or feel unsteady, ask a doctor or physiotherapist first.

How often should seniors do balance exercises?

Expert guidance suggests that fall-prevention exercise works best when done regularly, often three or more sessions a week. Start with fewer sessions and build up slowly under advice.

What if my parent is afraid to stand?

Begin with the seated weight shifts and seated exercises. Fear of falling is common. Go slowly, stay beside them and build confidence before trying standing moves with support.

Can I do heel-to-toe standing without holding on?

Not without professional guidance. Keep holding a fixed counter, do not close your eyes, and ask a physiotherapist before making any balance exercise harder.

Which exercises are best if I cannot stand safely?

Start with seated weight shifts, seated marching and ankle circles. For anyone who is bedbound or recovering from surgery, a clinician should plan the exercises.
