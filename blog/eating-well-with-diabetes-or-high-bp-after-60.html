Diabetes and BP

Eating Well With Diabetes or High BP After 60: General Guidance

Sarita NawarCertified Nutrition & Fitness Coach
8 min readUpdated 12 Nov 2026

The short answer

  • This is general guidance only. Your doctor decides your diet and medicines.
  • Eat regular, balanced Indian meals with fibre, protein and vegetables, and cut back on sugar and salt gradually.
  • Do not skip meals, starve yourself or stop medicines. Ask for a personalised plan if you have diabetes or high BP.

Diabetes and high blood pressure are very common after 60, and many families respond by cutting out half the food on the table. That can do more harm than good, leading to weakness, low blood sugar and unplanned weight loss. The aim is not a strict "diabetic diet" but a balanced, tasty Indian diet that fits your medicines and health. This guide gives general starting points. It does not replace your doctor.

First principles for seniors

  • Eat enough. Weight loss is not automatically healthy after 60. Protect muscle with enough protein and energy.
  • Keep meals regular and in similar amounts, so your medicines and blood sugar stay in step.
  • Individualise. The right plan depends on your medicines, kidneys, appetite, activity and preferences.
  • Move too. Walking and gentle strength work help both blood sugar and blood pressure.

Eating well with diabetes

  • Plan carbohydrate portions sensibly. Choose whole-wheat roti, millets, oats, brown or parboiled rice and mixed-grain atta.
  • Pair carbs with protein and vegetables, such as roti with dal and sabzi, for a steadier blood-sugar response.
  • Add fibre: vegetables, pulses, whole fruit and whole grains.
  • Limit sugary drinks, sweets, biscuits, maida and packaged snacks.
  • Do not skip meals, especially if you take insulin or tablets that can cause low sugar.
  • Large portion of white rice→A smaller portion of rice, with dal, vegetables and curd
  • Fruit juice→A whole fruit in a measured portion
  • Biscuits or rusk with chai→Roasted chana, a few nuts or sprouts
  • Sugary tea or sweets→Less sugar over time, with sweets only on special occasions
  • Maida-based foods→Whole-wheat, millet or mixed-grain atta

Low blood sugar: know the signs

Low blood sugar is the biggest risk for many older people with diabetes. Watch for sweating, shakiness, sudden hunger, dizziness, confusion or unusual behaviour. Ask your doctor for a written plan. A commonly used rule for someone who is awake and able to swallow is to take about 15 g of fast-acting sugar (for example glucose powder or sugary juice), then recheck in 15 minutes. If the person is drowsy, unconscious or cannot swallow safely, call for emergency help.

Eating well with high blood pressure

  • Cut salt gradually. The WHO suggests less than 5 g of salt a day, about 1 teaspoon. Your doctor may set a lower limit.
  • Fill up on vegetables, fruit, pulses and minimally processed foods.
  • Use oils in small amounts. Avoid vanaspati and deep-fried snacks.
  • Watch potassium: banana, coconut water, potato, spinach and pulses supply it. Ask your doctor first if you have kidney disease or take certain BP medicines.
  • Check blood pressure regularly, and avoid tobacco and excess alcohol.

Sarita's tip 🌿

Change one thing a week. First reduce salt at the table, then swap fruit juice for whole fruit, then add a vegetable to each meal. Small changes last longer than a strict new diet.

Medicines, timing and "miracle cures"

  • Take medicines exactly as prescribed, and match meal timing to your doctor's advice.
  • Carry an up-to-date list of your medicines.
  • Do not replace medicines with karela juice, herbal powders or supplements. Tell your doctor about everything you take.
  • If your appetite is poor or you are losing weight without trying, see your doctor. Do not try to lose weight.

Questions to ask your doctor or dietitian

  • How many carbohydrate portions suit me at each meal?
  • What time should I eat in relation to my tablets or insulin?
  • What salt limit is right for me?
  • Do my kidneys or medicines mean I should limit potassium or protein?
  • What should I do if my sugar or blood pressure is too low or too high?

For sons and daughters

Help read food labels, keep a record of sugar and blood pressure readings, and make sure your parent eats regularly. Do not restrict their food on your own. See our guides on the Indian Senior Plate, protein for seniors and hydration.

Get urgent medical help if

  • Confusion, drowsiness or fainting, or very low or very high blood sugar that does not settle.
  • Chest pain, severe breathlessness or a severe headache with very high blood pressure.
  • Sudden weakness on one side, a drooping face or difficulty speaking. Call 112 or your local ambulance number.

Frequently asked questions

Can seniors with diabetes eat roti and rice?

Usually yes, in sensible portions. Choose whole-wheat roti, millets, brown or parboiled rice, pair them with dal and vegetables, and spread meals through the day. Your doctor or dietitian can set portions that suit your medicines.

Is fruit allowed in diabetes?

Whole fruit in measured portions is usually fine and gives fibre. Choose whole fruit instead of juice, and ask your doctor how much suits you.

How much salt is right with high blood pressure?

The WHO suggests less than 5 g of salt a day, about 1 teaspoon, for adults. Your doctor may set a lower limit. Remember that pickles, papad, namkeen and packaged foods add hidden salt.

Can I stop my medicines if my diet improves?

No. Never stop or change prescribed medicines on your own. Good food can help your treatment, but only your doctor can decide changes to medicines.

Should I skip meals to control blood sugar?

No. Skipping meals can cause low blood sugar in people on certain medicines and can lead to weakness and weight loss. Eat regular meals at consistent times, as your doctor advises.

Do karela juice or herbal powders cure diabetes or BP?

No food, juice or herbal powder replaces prescribed medicines. Some products can interact with medicines or be unsafe. Tell your doctor about anything you take.
