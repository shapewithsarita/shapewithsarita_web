The 30-Second Chair Stand Test: Check Leg Strength at Home

Sarita NawarCertified Nutrition & Fitness Coach
7 min readUpdated 31 Oct 2026

The short answer

  • Count how many times you can stand up from a chair in 30 seconds with your arms crossed.
  • Do it only if you can stand safely, with a sturdy chair and someone nearby.
  • Repeat monthly and compare mainly with your own results.

Can you stand up from a chair without pushing on your hands? That simple action says a lot about leg strength, which matters for climbing stairs, getting up from the floor and staying steady. The 30-second chair stand test is an easy way to check leg strength at home and track progress over time. This guide shows how to do it safely, what the scores mean and how to improve.

What the test measures

The test checks leg strength and endurance. Doctors and physiotherapists use it, including in the CDC's STEADI fall-risk programme, as a quick screening check. It is not a diagnosis. Think of it as a useful starting point for a conversation with your doctor.

Is it safe for you?

Do this test only if you can stand up from a chair safely. Please check with your doctor first if any of these apply:

  • You have fallen recently or feel unsteady.
  • You feel dizzy or faint when you stand.
  • You have chest discomfort or unusual breathlessness.
  • You have had recent surgery, a fracture or a serious illness.
  • You have knee, hip or back pain that gets worse on standing.

What you need

  • A sturdy chair with a straight back, no armrests and no wheels, with a seat about 17 inches (43 cm) high. A normal dining chair is often close.
  • A wall behind the chair, so it cannot slide back.
  • A phone timer or clock with a second hand.
  • Closed, non-slip shoes.
  • A helper who stands beside you and counts.

How to do the test

  1. SitSit in the middle of the chair with your feet flat on the floor and your back straight.
  2. Cross armsCross your arms over your chest, with your hands on opposite shoulders.
  3. PractiseTry one or two slow stands first to check that it feels safe.
  4. GoWhen your helper says "Go" and starts the timer, stand fully up, then sit down. Repeat for 30 seconds.
  5. CountCount each full stand. If you are more than halfway up when time ends, count it.
  6. RecordWrite down your number and the date in your tracker.

If you need your hands to push up, stop. In the standard test this scores zero. Do not force it, and speak to your doctor or physiotherapist.

What your score means

The CDC lists the scores below as below average for each age. A below-average score suggests a higher risk of falls. These figures come from US research, so treat them as a rough guide.

A low score is not a diagnosis, and a good score does not mean you can skip safety. Your own trend matters most: aim to improve a little over time. The book recommends comparing your score only with your own earlier results, not with other people.

How to improve your score

  • Practise sit-to-stand: start with 5 to 8 slow repetitions, one set, 2 or 3 days a week. Use a higher seat or armrests if needed, and build toward 8 to 12 if it feels right.
  • Add supported heel raises and seated knee extensions to strengthen the legs.
  • Keep walking a little every day.
  • Eat enough protein from dal, curd, paneer, eggs, fish or chicken, spread across meals.
  • Keep going for at least 6 to 12 weeks before judging results.

How often to test

Once a month is enough. Use the same chair, the same time of day and the same method each time. Skip the test if you feel tired, unwell, dizzy or in pain.

Frequently asked questions

Is the 30-second chair stand test a good check of fall risk?

It is a simple screening test of leg strength and endurance, used in fall-risk checks such as the CDC STEADI programme. It does not diagnose anything, so a low score is a reason to talk to a doctor or physiotherapist, not to worry.

What is a good score for my age?

The CDC lists below-average scores by age and sex, shown in the table above. Those figures come from US research, so use them as a rough guide and compare mainly with your own earlier results.

Can I do the test alone?

It is safer with someone standing nearby, especially the first time. Do it only if you can stand up from a chair safely, with a sturdy chair against a wall.

What if I need to use my hands to stand up?

In the standard test, a person who must use their hands to stand scores zero and stops. Do not force it. Practise sit-to-stand with hand support, and speak to your doctor or physiotherapist.

How often should I repeat the test?

Once a month is enough for most people. Use the same chair, the same time of day and the same method, so you can compare results fairly.

What should I do if my score is low?

Do not panic. Start gentle leg-strengthening exercises such as sit-to-stand and heel raises, eat enough protein, keep walking, and ask your doctor or physiotherapist for advice, especially if you have fallen or feel unsteady.
